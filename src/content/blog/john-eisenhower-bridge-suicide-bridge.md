Tucked behind the Eisenhower Farm on Red Rock Road, just a short walk from the Sachs Covered Bridge, an unassuming iron truss bridge spans the quiet waters of Willoughby Run. By daylight, it's easy to miss — a relic of 19th-century engineering, overgrown at the edges, closed to vehicle traffic. By night, it's known by a different name entirely: Suicide Bridge.

This is the site of our debut investigation, and it's one of the most storied haunted locations in the Gettysburg area.

## The History Behind the Bridge

The John Eisenhower Bridge — officially Adams County Bridge #122 — was built in 1886 by the firm Gilbert & Smith. It's a Pratt-through truss design, notable for using pipe rather than solid beams for its struts and portal bracing, an unusual construction choice for its era. The bridge takes its name from John Eisenhower, son of President Dwight D. Eisenhower, an Army officer, military historian, and U.S. Ambassador to Belgium.

Today the bridge is limited to foot and bicycle traffic. It was heavily damaged by flooding in June 1996 and rebuilt in 1998, with the deck later refurbished by Adams County in 2012. Structurally, it has survived. Reputationally, it's known for something else.

## Why "Suicide Bridge"?

Local legend holds that the bridge earned its grim nickname from stories of despair playing out at the site — including a tale of a man who received divorce papers and later died at the bridge, his car reportedly found nearby with the papers still on the seat. Another version of the local lore tells of a woman who lost her husband in World War II. Like much of Gettysburg's oral history, these stories have circulated for generations without a clean paper trail, and we treat them the way we treat all local legend: as context, not confirmed fact.

What isn't in dispute is the bridge's reputation. It draws ghost tours, paranormal investigators, and curious visitors from well beyond Adams County, all looking for the same thing we were: an answer to whether something lingers there.
