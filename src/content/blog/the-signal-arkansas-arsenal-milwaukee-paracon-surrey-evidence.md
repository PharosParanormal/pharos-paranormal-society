No single blockbuster story this week, but a good spread of exactly what we like to cover: a genuinely old, genuinely documented haunted building, a long-running community gathering that's still growing after eleven years, and an independent team doing the unglamorous, patient work of sharing its case files with the public. No notable new ghost-hunting gear hit the market this week, so we're skipping the equipment section rather than stretching for one — every claim below is sourced, with links at the bottom.

## A 180-Year-Old Arsenal Tower, an EVP, and a Theory About Séances

The Tower Building at Little Rock's MacArthur Museum of Arkansas Military History has a résumé most haunted locations would envy. Built in 1840 as Arkansas's first arsenal, it stayed under Confederate control through the Civil War's early years until Union forces recaptured it in 1863. In 1864, 17-year-old David Dodd was hanged nearby after being caught spying for the Confederacy — he's one of several presences said to still linger on the grounds. The arsenal was decommissioned in 1890, and General Douglas MacArthur was born there in 1880, decades before it became the military history museum that bears his name. In 1942, Bernie Babcock founded a predecessor museum in the building and reportedly held séances in the basement — a detail some believe opened a door that never quite closed.

Staff and visitors have long reported the sound of boxes being moved with no one around to move them, along with sightings of a woman in a white dress on a staircase landing. Museum director Stephen McAteer offers a fairly grounded theory for why: "All the relics... guns and everything it's in the museum right now... all that carries energy." Rhonda Burton, co-founder of the local group Arkansas Ghost Catchers, investigated the tower and came away with an EVP recording that appears to say "Get out." "When I heard that I was shocked," she said. On the séance theory specifically, she's more measured: "People believe that that's what's happened." The museum leans into its reputation rather than downplaying it — its Paranormal Expo, a fundraiser running since 2011, returns October 24–25.

*We appreciate a haunted-location story that comes with an actual documented timeline instead of vague "something happened here once" lore. A 186-year-old building that's been a weapons arsenal, a hanging site, a séance parlor, and now a military history museum has had a lot of chances to pick up residents, and the people investigating it are refreshingly careful about separating the history from the EVP.*

## Milwaukee's ParaCon Hits Its Eleventh Year, Still Free, Still Packed

Milwaukee's ParaCon — the city's annual paranormal conference — drew more than 200 people to Kochanski's Concertina Beer Hall on October 17 for its eleventh consecutive year, according to Milwaukee Magazine. It's run by Mike Huberty, who inherited the event in 2015 from founder Tea Krulos; Huberty's sister, Allison Jornlin, founded Milwaukee Ghost Tours back in 2008 and used this year's conference to debut her new local ghost story collection, *In the Shadow of City Hall*. The day opened with an audience open mic where attendees could share their own ghost stories, ran about 15 vendors selling divination sets and macabre art, and closed out in an outdoor beer garden. It's free to attend, ticketed only through Eventbrite to manage the crowd. Huberty's own description of the day: "a full day of weirdness, and it's free."

*Eleven straight years of a free, volunteer-run paranormal conference surviving and growing is its own kind of evidence — not of ghosts, necessarily, but of how durable the actual community around this stuff is once you get past the TV-show version of it.*

## A B.C. Investigation Team Opens Its Files to the Public for Charity

Northern Paranormal Investigations (NPI), a British Columbia-based team founded by Darryl Pearson in 2008, held a public event on October 3 called "A Night of Paranormal Oddities & Evidence" at Alex House (Camp Alexandra) in Crescent Beach, South Surrey — their first time presenting at that location. Tickets were $10, with proceeds going to Alex House, a local service organization that now occupies the roughly 110-year-old camp property. The evening opened with the site's own history, moved into an explanation of how the team's investigations actually work, and then walked the audience through 16 years of accumulated NPI evidence — audio, video, and photographs from the camp itself and from other locations around the province — before closing with a roundtable Q&A where attendees could share their own experiences.

The team, which also includes investigators Mike Lutke and Aimee Bucholtz, was candid in interviews with Today in BC about what it is and isn't trying to do. Pearson said the group tries "to help people that need that help" and prioritizes finding logical explanations over claiming paranormal ones. Lutke said part of the goal is correcting misconceptions about what people assume is paranormal activity in the first place. Bucholtz, who has roughly two decades of investigative experience, said the team's mix of skeptics, believers, and non-believers is what keeps its work grounded.

*This is the kind of story we go looking for every week and don't always find: a team that's been quietly doing this for 16 years, is willing to show its work in public, and talks about debunking as often as it talks about confirming. It's a useful contrast to the reality-TV version of the field, and it's good to see it tied to an actual charitable cause.*
