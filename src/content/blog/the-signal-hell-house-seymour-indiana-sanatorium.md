## Hell House, Reopened: A Comedian and a Ghost-Hunting YouTuber Buy Connecticut's Warren Case

FOX61 News in Connecticut reported this week that the house known as "Hell House" — a 19th-century home at 89 Buckingham Road in Seymour — has new owners with a plan to turn it into a paranormal destination. The Beckwith family moved in back in 1998 and reported escalating activity, including claims that their children were attacked by unseen forces. They eventually brought in Ed and Lorraine Warren, and the investigation reportedly included exorcisms. The house became nationally known through a 2005 episode of the series *A Haunting* titled "Hell House."

The new owners are Elton Castee, a paranormal investigator and content creator behind the "Overnight" channel (he and his partners already own the Warren House in nearby Monroe, Connecticut), and comedian Matt Rife. Castee says someone connected to the property approached them about buying it; he wasn't looking to add another house at the time. He visited first, spending a night there with other creators while filming for someone else's channel — he says people in the group left one by one after a ball was pushed off the top of the stairs, and that a gas leak later developed in the basement. ("The house legitimately tried to kill us," he told FOX61.) Those specific claims are his own account and aren't independently documented in the report.

The plan is to fill the house with investigation equipment so overnight guests can document their own experiences, with each room's design tied to details from the 2005 episode: a red-toned "Red Room" referencing the family's account that their son was once locked there and used a knocking method to communicate, a "spiritual sanctuary" with a nod to the green toy flashlight the family used to comfort their youngest child, a kitchen table inspired by the Warrens' investigation, and a basement turned into a séance room. Overnight stays began November 5, starting at $666 a night. "We give everyone everything they could ever need to film, and that way if anything does happen, you can have it documented," Castee said. "If we can bring life back into this and allow other people to experience it, that's like why we do this."

*We'll take Castee at his word that the gas leak and stairs incident happened the way he describes, but we'd also point out that "the house tried to kill us" is exactly the kind of claim a soon-to-open paranormal B&B has every incentive to make. That doesn't mean the house isn't worth investigating — a documented exorcism case with a 20-plus-year paper trail is genuinely rare — it just means we'd treat the opening-week marketing copy and the actual history as two different things.*

## Indiana's "Shadow Hallway": A Sanatorium's History Gets a Second Look

WLFI News 18 in Lafayette, Indiana spent a night at the Indiana State Sanatorium in Rockville for its ongoing "Hoosier Haunts" series, published November 3. The sprawling property opened as a tuberculosis hospital, later became a nursing home, and still has a coal power plant on its grounds. Steve Cecchin, business manager with the Sanatorium Project — the preservation group that now runs the site — told WLFI that visitors and investigators have reported shadow figures moving room to room through a wing of the former nursing home that's picked up the nickname "Shadow Hallway," along with taps on the shoulder, unexplained scratches people have shown him in photos, and a staircase at the base of the old TB hospital where, he says, a disproportionate number of women report feeling sick or uncomfortable.

Cecchin also pointed to two pieces of the property's history that may feed the reputation: an engineer at the old coal plant who's said to have shot a co-worker and then himself, and an account — which Cecchin flagged himself as something he'd simply heard, not documentation — of a patient with schizophrenia who reportedly died after telling others about a ghostly nurse on the ward. "The word paranormal means we can't explain it," Cecchin told the station. "Many people have felt like a tap on the shoulder."

*Credit to Cecchin and to WLFI for the distinction, because it's one a lot of haunted-location coverage skips: a documented tragedy on a property is one thing, and a secondhand story about a ghost someone heard about from someone else is another, and the report is honest about which is which. A former TB hospital and nursing home has a real, heavy history on its own merits — it doesn't need an embellished version to be worth preserving or worth visiting respectfully.*

## Gear Watch: A Metronome Rejoins the Field Kit

GhostStop is currently selling a device called Dead Time, billed as "the vintage metronome for ghost communication" — a traditional wind-up metronome body with a built-in EMF sensor wired into the pendulum. The idea: set a sensitivity threshold, and when the sensor picks up a magnetic-field fluctuation above it, the swinging pendulum physically stops and the unit's lights flash before it releases and resumes ticking, which GhostStop frames as a yes/no trigger object (left for yes, right for no, for example). It also works as a plain mechanical metronome with the power off. It's listed at $239.95, marked down from $279.95, with the black version in limited stock and the wood version already sold out. GhostStop pitches it alongside its existing Dead Bell and Spirit Candle as part of a deliberate return to old-fashioned, low-tech trigger objects rather than another screen-and-sensor box.

We couldn't pin down an exact launch date for Dead Time — GhostStop's product page carries no date, just a "NEW!" label and an "Intro Price" — so we're flagging it honestly as a current listing rather than breaking news. If you've used one in the field, we'd like to hear about it.
