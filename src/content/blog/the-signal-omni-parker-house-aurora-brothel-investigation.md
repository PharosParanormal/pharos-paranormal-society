No "ordinary person" unexplained-experience story made the cut again this week. The closest thing we found was a TikTok series from a self-described Army veteran documenting activity in an old house, but there's no way to confirm his identity, his service, or any of the historical claims layered into the videos — it's built entirely to be shared, not verified, so we're leaving it out. What we do have: a Boston landmark's haunted reputation getting a fresh, well-sourced look, a Missouri news crew's own overnight investigation at a 19th-century brothel, and a trigger device that turned out to be older than its "NEW" tag claims. Every claim below is sourced, with links at the bottom.

## A Night (or Several Generations) at the Omni Parker House

The Huntington News — Northeastern University's student newspaper — published a deep dive this month into the haunted reputation of Boston's Omni Parker House, the 1855 hotel on the Freedom Trail where Charles Dickens reportedly roomed for about five months, Ho Chi Minh and Malcolm X are both said to have worked in the same kitchen that perfected the Boston cream pie, and JFK is said to have proposed to Jacqueline Bouvier in the dining room.

The hotel's ghost stories cluster around a few recurring details. Susan Wilson, the Parker House's historian for 20 years, told the paper that guests on the 10th floor have long reported a shadowy figure in the hallways and at the foot of their beds — one they associate with founder Harvey Parker himself. The bigger cluster of stories centers on the third floor and Room 303 in particular, where Wilson says a heavy-drinking, chain-smoking man died sometime in the mid-20th century; guests on that floor have reported smelling alcohol or cigarette smoke with no source, and some say the elevator stops there even when another floor is selected. Wilson, for her part, says she's never personally experienced anything paranormal in 20 years on the job, though she's open-minded about it: "There is a great deal of evidence that paranormal activity has occurred here. Luckily, it's all very friendly."

Two guest accounts round out the piece. Vinh Trinh, who stayed at the hotel in 2025, says he smelled cigarette smoke throughout his stay on a floor where smoking wasn't allowed, and that a fire alarm went off only in his husband's room — with the front desk reportedly telling them no alarm was on record. Kelly Briggs, visiting from Wisconsin with her child, said that after photographing Room 303's door, the room number didn't show up in the photo afterward, though a painting of a horse hanging above it did. The Huntington News also reports that all 10 guests on a late-October historical tour led by Wilson said they experienced something they considered paranormal, mostly on the third floor.

*Ten guests on a guided ghost tour reporting "something paranormal" afterward isn't the same as ten guests independently reporting it cold — a tour is, almost by design, primed to produce exactly that result, and the article doesn't pretend otherwise. What we like about this one is that the hotel's own historian is the most skeptical person quoted in it. A 170-year-old hotel with this much real history (Dickens, Kennedy, a Vietnamese revolutionary's day job) doesn't need embellishment to be worth a visit — the ghost stories are just the part that gets people talking.*

## Seven Hours Inside a Missouri Brothel-Turned-Antique-Shop

KY3, the NBC affiliate in Springfield, Missouri, wrapped up a two-part "Ozarks Life" series this fall following paranormal investigator David Glidden — host of the YouTube series "Haunting History" — and his friend John Long through an overnight investigation of a building in Aurora, Missouri that local tour guide Dalton Harris (of Show Me Something Spooky) says was "confirmed" as a brothel as of 1892. The building is now an antique shop, run for the last ten years by owner Myra Goodson, who told KY3, "There's always activity upstairs, there's walking." Harris says newspaper research turned up charges filed against two women who worked there, one of whom, Nora Henry, died on the property.

Over roughly seven hours, the team logged a handful of specific moments rather than one big reveal. In the basement, a bell-based trigger device rang repeatedly with no one near it — Glidden described it as responding to static-field changes. On the main floor, once a saloon and gambling hall, reporter Chad Plein said something poked him in the back while the group played poker; Glidden's response was blunt about the limits of that kind of evidence: "If you believe in that experience, you just had something that not very many people have happened... But a personal experience isn't evidence." Upstairs, on the floor that once housed the brothel itself, the team heard unexplained footsteps and used a MicroKom — a device that scans rapidly through radio frequencies — to ask questions; asked who ran the day shift, it returned something Glidden and Plein interpreted as "manager," and asked how many women worked there during the day, it returned "thirteen." Glidden's own framing: "Could this have been coincidence? Potentially. But it's a very interesting coincidence." Near a bed the shop keeps under camera because its sheet keeps turning up disturbed, the sheet was clean when the team arrived and had lint on it by the time they left, with no obvious airflow source nearby to account for it.

The series also notes that another group, Southwest Detroit Paranormal, visited the shop earlier this year and came away believing they'd photographed a shadowy figure near that same bed.

*What stands out here is Glidden policing his own evidence out loud — "a personal experience isn't evidence" is a stricter standard than most TV ghost hunting bothers to state on camera, and he applies it to his own team's claims, not just the audience's skepticism. None of this proves the building is haunted. It does show what a genuinely cautious investigation looks like when the cameras are rolling: specific, modest claims, openly flagged uncertainty, and a group willing to say "maybe it's a coincidence" out loud.*

## Gear Watch: A Trigger Object That's Older Than Its "New" Label

GhostStop currently lists the Tracer 2 Light Wire with a "NEW Model!" badge, so we went looking into what it actually does — and ran into a wrinkle worth flagging honestly. The device pairs a base unit using infrared LiDAR to sense motion and distance with an 8.5-foot detachable rope-light "wire" laid out in a line from it: per GhostStop's own description, the wire lights up white while it detects motion, then switches to red afterward to trace the path taken, and a newer firmware adds a color cue for temperature swings — blue for a cold spike, purple for a warm one. The pitch is that because it relies on LiDAR rather than EMF or radio sensing, only an actual physical obstruction should trigger it, in theory cutting down on the false positives other trigger objects are prone to.

Here's the catch: GhostStop's own blog post introducing the Tracer 2 is dated back to June 2024 — more than two years before that "NEW Model!" badge is still showing up on the product page today. We've flagged this pattern before with other GhostStop listings (the Onvoy 2 and Dead Time, covered in recent weeks, both carry no confirmed launch date either), and this is the clearest case yet of a "new" tag outliving the product's actual release by a wide margin. It's listed at $259.95, on sale for $199.95. We still think the underlying idea — swapping EMF/radio sensing for a LiDAR-based trigger wire — is a genuinely interesting piece of engineering worth knowing about. We just can't call it new, and neither, really, can GhostStop's own site.
