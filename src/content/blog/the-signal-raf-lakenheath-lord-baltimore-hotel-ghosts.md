No "ordinary person" unexplained-experience story made the cut this week, and no new gear, either — we went looking for both, same as always, and came up with nothing but 2020–2024 reruns dressed up as current (a Michigan nanny-cam story, a "28 Days Haunted" equipment piece from years back, a Dead Bell v2 review that's actually from December 2024). We'd rather hand you two real, freshly reported stories than pad the post with a third that doesn't hold up. Every claim below is sourced, with links at the bottom.

## Lakenheath Charlie: The Airman Who Never Left His Post

Stars and Stripes, the U.S. military's own editorially independent newspaper, published a deep dive this week into the ghost stories attached to RAF Lakenheath — a Royal Air Force station in England that now hosts the largest U.S. Air Force presence in the country. The base was first stood up as a decoy airfield for nearby RAF Mildenhall early in World War II and became a permanent installation in 1941, which means it's had plenty of time to accumulate a reputation.

The best-known legend is "Lakenheath Charlie," a WWII-era airman said to walk the airfield at night carrying a red lantern, waving off incoming pilots as if he never got word the war ended. Airmen also report him turning up at hangars after dark, asking for a cigarette, and vanishing before anyone can answer. Darren Mann, who runs a paranormal-sightings database covering the UK and Ireland, told the paper that unverified reports at Lakenheath go back to the 1940s — including a 1945 case (his file No. 59) of a figure seen crossing the airfield on moonlit nights, which Mann attributes to an Australian pilot whose bomber crashed there during the war, and a 1951 case (No. 317) in which an American military policeman picked up a lone British pilot walking along the base's Perimeter Road, only for the passenger to disappear from the vehicle before they arrived anywhere.

The more recent reports come with names attached. Around 2000–2001, two airmen told Stars and Stripes they'd watched security camera footage of a woman in white running along the inside of the base's perimeter fence; security forces and a canine unit searched and found no one. Sgt. Richard Mason has described hearing an unexplained whirring and rumbling from an empty building on base one night in 1980, with no machinery or anyone around to account for it. And Pvt. Edward Smith reported feeling a presence behind him while walking to his quarters in 1967 — nothing visible, but a sense that stuck with him for days.

*We don't get many haunted-location stories where the "location" is an active military installation still running real operations, which makes this one unusually interesting — these aren't tourists or investigators chasing a reputation, they're service members reporting things to each other on a base they can't just decide to stop visiting. That doesn't make any of it paranormal. But a legend that's been independently reported across eight decades, by people with no ticket to sell and no show to pitch, is a different kind of data point than most of what crosses our desk.*

## A Baltimore Hotel's Hidden Room

Closer to home, FOX45 (WBFF) in Baltimore sent a reporter and a paranormal investigator into the historic Lord Baltimore Hotel this week for an overnight look at the building's reputation. Vince Wilson, a Baltimore-based paranormal researcher, led the walkthrough; Lee Johnson-Lowe, the hotel's director of marketing, spoke to the guest reports the property fields regularly.

The investigation worked through a few specific claims: sounds of a party — clinking glasses, singing — in the hotel's old speakeasy space, which Wilson said is a common pattern for buildings with that kind of history; and staff accounts, on the 19th floor, of an 8-year-old girl named Molly who's said to play with a red bouncy ball in the hallways. (The backstory offered for Molly — that her family lost everything in the 1929 stock market crash — is presented in the piece without any documentation behind it, so we'd treat that detail as hotel lore rather than established history.) While investigating that floor, the team found a sealed-off room that isn't part of the hotel's public tour. The night wrapped with an unexplained noise Wilson himself declined to oversell, saying it could have been a ghost or could have been the building settling. The hotel's own position, per Johnson-Lowe: nobody's ever been hurt there, whatever guests think they've heard.

*This is a pretty ordinary example of a format we see constantly — a local news crew, a credentialed local investigator, one night, no dramatic reveal — and we mean that as a compliment. Wilson naming "the building settling" as a live possibility, out loud, in the same breath as "ghost," is exactly the kind of honesty that's rarer than it should be in this field. A hidden room nobody mentions on the tour is a legitimately interesting find regardless of what is or isn't making noise in it.*
